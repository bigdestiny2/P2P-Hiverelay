1In one minute

Most apps live on a company’s servers. If that company blocks you, goes down, or changes the rules, you’re stuck.

Peer-to-peer (P2P) apps try something else: your phone and laptop talk more directly, and you hold your data (with crypto keys), not a giant central database.

But pure P2P has a silly-sounding problem: when everyone goes to sleep, the app disappears.

This project builds always-on helper computers (“relays”) that keep apps online — without needing those helpers to own or (for private stuff) read your content. On top of that, we’re shipping real apps — especially Peerit, a Reddit-like social app.

Kitchen analogy Your recipe book is the app. Your friends’ kitchens are other users. A relay is a 24/7 community fridge that can hold a copy of the public cookbook — or a locked box only you have the key for — so dinner still happens when you’re out.

2The problem (shown, not just told)

Two different internet problems stack on top of each other.

A) Big platforms are single switches

One company runs the servers, the login, the feed, the ban hammer. Convenient — until the switch flips (outage, ban, price change, government order).

B) Pure P2P falls asleep

If the only copy of an app lives on the creator’s laptop, closing that laptop is like unplugging the website.

Figure 1 — Pure P2P: when the publisher sleeps, the product can vanish for everyone else.

3The fix: always-on helpers called relays

A HiveRelay is a computer that stays online and acts like an extra peer. It can keep a copy of public app data, help people connect, and (for private data) hold locked boxes it can’t open.

Figure 2 — Relays are just peers that don’t sleep. The creator can go offline; the app stays reachable.

❌ Old “just host it on our server”

  • Company owns the database
  • You hope they stay nice
  • One ban = everything gone

✓ Relay as a peer with uptime

  • Keys identify content, not “account rows”
  • You can check proofs (not only trust marketing)
  • Another relay can replace a bad one

One important idea The identity of an app is a cryptographic key (think: a unique fingerprint), not a domain name and not a relay brand. Domains and relays are doors. The key is the house number that never moves.

7Peerit — a social app without “the company database”

Peerit feels a bit like Reddit: communities, posts, comments, votes. Under the hood it’s very different.

Figure 6 — Peerit: many personal signed notebooks + client merge — not one company spreadsheet of all posts.

  • You sign what you write — forgery fails crypto checks.
  • Devices merge by rules — not by “whatever the server says.”
  • Moderation is signed data too — not an invisible admin rewrite of history.
  • Relays help availability for browsers via the OutboxLog service (see catalog above).
Honest status Peerit is live as a public writable canary (real users can post). That is not the same as “fully multi-operator, marketing-scale GA.” We’re still hardening capacity, extra relays, and polish.

8Two different doors (don’t mix them up)

People often mash “HTTPS website” and “private encrypted storage” into one idea. We intentionally keep them separate.

Figure 7 — Public gateway ≠ privacy product. Blind cells ≠ “any HTTPS link is private.”

Door A — Public gateway

  • Goal: open public P2P apps in normal Chrome/Safari
  • Serves exact public files
  • Hostname is replaceable
  • No privacy brag

Door B — Blind cells

  • Goal: store opaque encrypted slots
  • App (e.g. Peerit later) encrypts on the device
  • Relay shouldn’t learn post text
  • Still in active development

9Blind substrate: one warehouse, thousands of apps & users

The next-generation storage path is deliberately app-agnostic. Relays do not install “the Peerit plugin” for every new social app. They only store locked boxes (cells), opaque inboxes, and receipts.

Figure 8 — Scaling model for privacy path: N apps × M users share relays; apps never need a relay-side install.

Why this scales to “many apps”

  • No per-app daemon code — supporting a new app is a client change, not a fleet upgrade.
  • No semantic indexes on the relay — posts, shops, tickets stay inside ciphertext.
  • Quotas & admission — each relay sells capacity (bytes, ops), not “Peerit seats.”
  • Horizontal growth — add more relay operators; clients pick several for copies (G2-S multi-replica).
  • Peerit is customer #1, not the protocol owner — two fixture apps prove the substrate isn’t Peerit-shaped.

Honest ceiling The architecture is built for multi-app, multi-user scale. The live Peerit path today is still OutboxLog on a small bootstrap fleet. Blind cells are a large draft on the vNext train — the design supports thousands; production claims wait for freeze, soak, and dual-read migration.

Scale rules of thumb (friendly)

  • Availability scales with replicas — more independent operators, not one fatter server.
  • Trust scales with signatures — verifying a receipt does not get slower just because the planet has more relays.
  • Per-node costs stay bounded — storage caps, eviction, rate limits, AutoHeal proof budgets.
  • Direct P2P first — relays are the always-on backup path, not a forced middlebox for every byte.
  • Year-1 ambition (planning, not promise) — ecosystem report sketches thousands of operators via home nodes + Lightning funnel; bootstrap starts from founder fleet + appliances.
